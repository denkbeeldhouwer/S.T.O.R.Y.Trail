# Carving Spoons at the Fire

**Green wood, slow hands, and the stories that come loose when the mouth is not in charge**

*Session proposal — Wild Pedagogies Gathering 2026*
*Format: campfire session · 2 hours · any evening, and repeatable across evenings*
*Facilitator: Guido Crolla (Denkbeeldhouwer, Arnhem)*

---

## What this is

We sit around the fire and carve spoons from fresh wood. By the end of the evening most people are holding something they can eat with.

That is the whole session. Everything else happens by itself.

## Why a spoon

Green wood is wet, soft, and alive. It cuts differently along the grain than across it, it splits where a branch once grew, and it warps a little as it dries. You cannot impose a shape on it. You can only find the spoon that the piece of wood is already willing to become — which usually means letting go of the spoon you had in mind.

An hour of that does something to a person that no lecture on more-than-human agency can do. The wood decides. You negotiate. The distribution of agency stops being a research question and becomes the thing in your hands.

The material comes from what would otherwise be waste: prunings, storm wood, thinnings. In my own practice this is a principle rather than a convenience — what a place cuts away in autumn becomes what it makes in spring.

## Why at the fire

Carving occupies the hands and the eyes but leaves the voice free, and it takes away the obligation to look at each other. In my experience this is the most reliable condition for honest conversation there is. Stories arrive sideways. Silences are comfortable, because everyone is busy. The session is a making session and a storytelling session at the same time, and I would not want to separate them.

I will bring one question to open with, and then let the fire and the knives do the rest.

## What people take home

A spoon that is slightly wrong, entirely theirs, and used every day. Care for the world is easier to sustain when it fits in a drawer.

## Practical

I bring carving knives and hook knives, plus fresh wood cut close to the site. My own set covers **four carvers per evening**, so I propose running the session on several evenings and reaching twelve or so people over the week. If the organisation is able to sponsor tools, I will gladly buy extra knives and take larger groups; the knives stay with the gathering afterwards.

Participants who own carving tools are warmly asked to bring them. A short instruction on grip and cutting direction comes first. Suitable for complete beginners — most people have a rough spoon by the end of the evening and refine it during the rest of the week. Anyone is welcome to sit down and watch, which is half the point of a fire anyway.
